How do I get rid of PMI?

If you put less than 20% down on a regular (conventional) loan, you're probably paying private mortgage insurance. It doesn't have to last forever, and plenty of people pay it longer than they need to.

The two dates that matter

  • At 80%, you can ask. Once your loan balance is down to 80% of the home's original value (usually the price you paid), you can ask your lender in writing to cancel PMI. You need a good payment record, no second mortgage, and the home can't have lost value.
  • At 78%, it comes off on its own. The lender has to end PMI on the date your balance is scheduled to hit 78% of the original value, as long as you're current on payments.

Those rules come from the federal Homeowners Protection Act. The 78% date is on the PMI disclosure in your closing papers.

Get there sooner

  • Extra principal payments move the 80% date up. Even a little each month adds up over a few years.
  • A higher home value. If your home is worth a lot more than you paid, many lenders will drop PMI based on a new appraisal. For loans backed by Fannie Mae, for example, that generally means owing 75% or less of the current value if you've had the loan 2 to 5 years, or 80% or less after 5 years. You usually pay for the appraisal. Call your lender and ask what they require.

FHA loans are different

FHA loans have mortgage insurance premiums (MIP) instead of PMI. For FHA loans started after June 2013, MIP lasts the life of the loan if you put down less than 10%, or 11 years if you put down 10% or more. The usual way out early is refinancing into a conventional loan once you have enough equity.

From the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance on removing PMI. Your lender has the final word on your loan.
